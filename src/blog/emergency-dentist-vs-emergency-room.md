When a tooth suddenly hurts, breaks, or gets knocked loose, it is not always obvious whether to call a dentist or head to the emergency room. The short version: most dental problems are handled faster, better, and less expensively by a dentist — but some symptoms are medical emergencies that need an ER or 911 right away.

Here is how to tell the difference.

## Go to the ER or Call 911 First If You Have Any of These

Some symptoms point to a spreading infection or serious injury that is beyond what a dental office can safely manage on its own:

- Swelling that affects your **breathing or swallowing**, or is spreading toward your eye or neck.
- **Uncontrolled bleeding** that does not slow with steady pressure.
- A **high fever** with facial swelling, or feeling severely unwell.
- **Facial trauma** with possible jaw fracture, or a head injury.
- Any symptom that feels **life-threatening**.

In these situations, the hospital can stabilize you, manage the airway, and provide IV antibiotics or urgent imaging. A dentist can follow up on the tooth afterward. When in doubt about breathing or a spreading infection, choose the ER.

## A Dentist Is Usually the Right Call For

For the large majority of dental emergencies, a dental office is both faster and more definitive, because the ER can typically only prescribe pain medication or antibiotics — it rarely treats the tooth itself. Call a dentist for:

- **Severe or worsening toothache**
- A **knocked-out, chipped, or broken tooth**
- A **lost filling or crown**
- A **dental abscess or gum boil** without the red-flag symptoms above
- **Pain around a wisdom tooth**
- A **loose adult tooth** or an injury that pushed a tooth out of position

The ER does not have a dentist on staff in most cases. You may wait hours, receive a prescription, and still be told to see a dentist — losing a day and paying far more in the process.

## A Knocked-Out Adult Tooth Is Time-Sensitive

If a permanent tooth is completely knocked out, the first hour matters. In general:

- Handle the tooth by the **crown**, not the root.
- If it is dirty, rinse it gently with milk or saline — do not scrub.
- If you can, **place it back in the socket** and bite gently on a clean cloth.
- If you cannot reinsert it, keep it in **milk** (or tucked inside the cheek for an adult who will not swallow it) and get to a dentist immediately.

The sooner the tooth is back in place, the better the chance of saving it.

## Managing Pain Until You Are Seen

While you arrange care, general comfort measures include over-the-counter pain relief used as directed on the label, a cold compress on the outside of the cheek for swelling, and avoiding very hot, cold, or hard foods on that side. Do not place aspirin directly on the gum, and do not ignore swelling that is getting worse.

This is general guidance, not a prescription — follow the label and your own medical circumstances, and ask a professional if you are unsure.

## Why This Matters for Cost, Too

Emergency rooms are built for medical emergencies and priced accordingly. For a problem that originates in a tooth, an ER visit often means a large bill for a temporary fix, followed by a dental visit anyway. Calling a dentist first usually resolves the actual problem in one place.

## The Simple Rule

Ask yourself one question: **is this a breathing, bleeding, or spreading-infection emergency?** If yes, go to the ER or call 911. If no, call a dentist promptly — the sooner you are seen, the more options you usually have.
