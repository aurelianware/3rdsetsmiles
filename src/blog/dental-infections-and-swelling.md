Most dental infections are treatable and not dangerous when addressed promptly. But a dental infection is still a real infection, and in some cases it can spread into spaces of the face and neck where it becomes a medical emergency. Knowing which signs mean "call the dentist soon" versus "get emergency care now" can genuinely matter.

## The Warning Signs That Mean Emergency Care Now

Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room if a dental infection comes with any of these:

- **Swelling that affects breathing or swallowing,** or a feeling that your throat is tightening
- Swelling that is **spreading toward your eye,** closing the eye, or moving down the neck
- **Difficulty opening your mouth** along with rapidly worsening swelling
- **High fever, chills, or feeling severely unwell**
- A **rapidly enlarging** area of swelling over hours

These can signal that an infection is moving beyond the tooth into deeper tissues, which needs hospital-level care — IV antibiotics, airway monitoring, and sometimes urgent drainage. When in doubt about breathing or fast-spreading swelling, do not wait.

## Signs of a Dental Infection That Needs Prompt (Not Emergency) Care

Without the red flags above, these symptoms still mean you should see a dentist quickly — usually within a day:

- A persistent, throbbing **toothache**
- A **pimple-like bump** on the gum (a "gum boil") that may drain a bad taste
- **Localized swelling or tenderness** around one tooth or the jaw
- **Sensitivity to pressure** when biting, or a tooth that feels "high"
- **Bad breath or a foul taste** that won't clear

An abscess will not heal on its own. Even if it drains and the pain eases for a while, the underlying infection remains and tends to return, often worse.

## Why "The Pain Went Away" Can Be Misleading

It is common for a severe toothache to suddenly stop. That can happen when the nerve inside the tooth dies — but the infection at the root does not stop with it. Relief without treatment is not the same as recovery. If you had significant pain that vanished, still get evaluated.

## What You Can Do Before You're Seen

While you arrange care for a non-emergency infection:

- **Rinse gently with warm salt water** a few times a day.
- **Use over-the-counter pain relief as directed** on the label.
- **Apply a cold compress** to the outside of the cheek for swelling — never heat.
- **Do not** try to pop or squeeze a gum boil, and **do not** place aspirin on the gum.
- **Do not** start leftover antibiotics on your own; the right treatment depends on the diagnosis, and antibiotics alone rarely resolve a dental abscess without treating the tooth.

## The Real Fix Is Treating the Source

Antibiotics may be part of care, but they manage the infection temporarily — they don't cure the cause. Lasting resolution usually means treating the tooth itself: a root canal to save it, or an extraction if it can't be saved, plus drainage of the abscess. That's why prompt dental evaluation matters even when medication brings short-term relief.

## Bottom Line

Take dental infections seriously. If there's swelling that affects breathing or swallowing, spreads toward the eye or neck, or comes with high fever, treat it as a medical emergency — ER or 911. For a painful tooth, gum boil, or localized swelling without those red flags, see a dentist promptly; the infection needs the source treated, not just the pain masked.
